\begin{abstract}
Decision makers seldom receive unlimited opportunities to revise a plan. We
study how much revision an adversarial trajectory planner needs when revisions
are rationed to a calendar: a public set of epochs at which the player may
observe the executed history and seal a new block of commands. The underlying
game is history dependent --- trailing vessels follow a leader's executed
track, so the current pose does not determine the formation --- and the payoff
integrates a directional interaction field along the trajectory.
We show that the value attainable with at most $K$ revisions, $W_K$, is
computable exactly up to a certified interval; that it is monotone in $K$ by
policy inclusion; and that the calendar objective is \emph{not} submodular, so
selecting revisions is not a greedy problem --- we give a binary-action
counterexample. Two certificates make budgets auditable: a constructive
deletion certificate that returns a feasible coarse policy and bounds its loss,
and an interval-loss certificate for a fully flexible opponent that reduces a
budget question to a shortest path over public intervals. On a frozen
computational benchmark of three geometries, four speed/distance
configurations, two opponent classes and three one-factor ablations, the
budget is binding rather than slack: in most cells the smallest budget that
attains the fully flexible value is the full budget, and where it is not, the
maximizing calendars concentrate on the early engaged epochs. We report the
sharpness of each certificate rather than presenting a valid bound as a tight
one, and we keep a previously tested hypothesis --- that the range-advantaged
side values revisions differently --- as a falsified result rather than
reviving it under a new name.
\end{abstract}
